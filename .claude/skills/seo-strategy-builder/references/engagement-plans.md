# The twelve-month plan, and the six-month one

Two things keep going wrong in the same place.

**The plan does not say it is a year.** A strategy arrives with a hundred
blog topics, sixteen service pages and a backlink programme, and nowhere does
it say when any of that happens or that the whole thing is twelve months of
work. The client reads it as a list of everything we are about to do, is
surprised in month two that only four posts exist, and is surprised again in
month nine that there is still work left.

**The six-month client gets the twelve-month plan.** Somebody trims it
afterwards, badly, and the deliverables no longer match what was sold.

So: the engagement length is decided before the strategy is written, and the
document is built for it.

---

## Which plan this client is on

`get_client_profile` returns `commercial.plan_terms` — a term in months per
service, set by the advisor when they activated it. **Read it before writing
anything.** Where it says 6, build the six-month plan; where it says 12 or is
absent, build the twelve-month plan.

Absent means twelve on purpose. Over-delivering against a six-month contract
is an awkward conversation; under-delivering against a twelve-month one is a
refund.

---

## What the client is buying, in one sentence

> This plan covers twelve months. The content and every other deliverable
> below is spread across that year, and we keep optimizing what is already
> live for the whole of it — the work does not stop once the pages are
> published.

That sentence, or its six-month equivalent, goes in the summary on page one.
The second half matters as much as the first: clients assume an SEO
engagement is a delivery project that ends when the last page ships, and the
part that compounds is the twelve months of revising what is already there.

---

## The twelve-row table

Front matter, straight after the priorities. **Twelve rows, one per month.**
Not a task list — the task board holds the individual rows, and a client
reading ninety of them learns nothing.

| Month | Focus | Content | Off-page | Technical & local |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Foundations | 2 service pages | Disavow audit, Tier 1 listings | Titles, meta, schema on existing pages |
| 2 | Core services | 3 service pages, 2 posts | Awards and chamber listings | Internal linking, URL changes |
| 3 | Local | 4 location pages, 2 posts | Anchor repair, micro-site decision | GBP optimization |
| … | | | | |
| 12 | Review and refresh | 2 posts, annual review | Quarterly anchor review | Content refresh on the top 10 pages |

Rules for the table:

- **Counts, not names.** "3 service pages" belongs here; which three belongs
  in the page plan. A client scanning twelve rows is asking how much and
  when, not which.
- **Every month has something in every column that applies.** A month with an
  empty Off-page cell is a month the client will ask about.
- **The last three rows are not padding.** Months 10 to 12 are where the
  refresh work, the case-study content and the annual review live. If they
  look thin, the plan has front-loaded the deliverables and the client will
  notice in month ten.
- **Add the counts up and check them against the deliverable list.** A table
  promising 40 posts beside a calendar holding 30 is the kind of error that
  survives three readings.

## What the six-month plan is

Not the twelve-month plan with months 7 to 12 deleted. The monthly rate is
lower too, so the per-month volumes drop as well as the total.

| | 12-month | 6-month |
|---|---|---|
| Months | 12 | 6 |
| Blog topics planned | 100 | 50 |
| Blog posts written | ~40 | ~15 |
| Service pages | Full set | The highest-priority half |
| City location pages | Full set | Top catchment only |
| Micro geo-location pages | 12–16 neighbourhoods | Not included |
| Resource guides | 3–4 | 1 |
| Backlinks | Tiers 1–3 cleared, then ongoing digital PR | Tiers 1–3 only |
| Content refresh | Quarterly | Not included |
| Competitor watch | Monthly | Not included |
| Reporting | Monthly, plus a quarterly retrospective | Monthly |

The six-month plan still gets the **whole** of the foundation: the technical
fixes, the titles and meta on existing pages, the URL changes, the disavow
audit, the Google Business Profile work, and the Tier 1–3 link checklist.
Those are finite, they are what everything else is built on, and halving them
would be selling a worse version of the same thing rather than less of it.

What it does not get is the compounding half — the long tail of content, the
digital PR that takes months to land, and the three extras below.

**The topic set is halved too, not just the writing.** A six-month plan is
built on fifty topics, not a hundred with thirty-five of them greyed out. The
clustering, the scoring and the target-page map all run over fifty. A
hundred-topic list handed to a six-month client is mostly a list of things
that will not happen, and it makes the plan read as a brochure for the
upgrade rather than as their plan. What they do get is a short, honest
section saying the year's plan runs to a hundred — see the comparison
section above.

**Say this in the document.** A six-month client reading their plan should be
able to see what the twelve-month plan adds, in one short section, written
without pressure. They bought six months; the honest thing is to tell them
what the year does differently and let them decide at renewal.

## The three twelve-month extras

All three are real work, none of them decides whether the client ranks. That
is the point: a premium feature that is load-bearing for results is not a
premium feature, it is something the six-month client is being denied.

### Competitor watch

A monthly note on what the named rivals did: pages they published, titles
they changed, phrases they started ranking for that they did not hold last
month, and links they picked up.

Why it earns its place: it is the section clients read first, it costs us
little because we already pull competitor keyword data for the strategy, and
it is the only part of a monthly report that is about somebody other than
them. A client who can see a rival publishing twice a month understands the
cadence argument without us making it.

Report it as: three to six specific changes, named, with the date. Not a
score, not a count — "they published *Bail Hearing Lawyer Mississauga* on 3
March and it is on page one for two of your tracked phrases" is the finding.

### Quarterly content refresh

Every quarter, the three best-performing older pages are rewritten rather
than left alone: facts checked, sections added where searches have moved,
internal links updated to point at pages that did not exist when it was
written, and the publish date updated honestly because the page genuinely
changed.

Why it earns its place: it is the work that only exists if the engagement
runs long enough to have older pages worth refreshing, and it reliably moves
pages that are stuck on page two. It is also the clearest answer to "what are
you doing now that everything is built", which is the question that ends
six-month engagements.

Not a date change. A page whose only edit is its timestamp is one of the
oldest tricks in the field and a client's developer can see it in the diff.

### Micro geo-location pages

Twelve to sixteen pages at **neighbourhood** level rather than city level:
*Criminal Defence Lawyer Casa Loma*, *Criminal Lawyer Wychwood*, *Criminal
Lawyer Oakwood Village* — the areas inside a city that people actually name
when they say where they are.

Why it earns its place: they catch the searches a city page cannot, because
somebody typing their own neighbourhood is further down the funnel than
somebody typing the city. They also compound — a neighbourhood page that
ranks keeps ranking, and there is no ongoing cost once it is live. And they
are genuinely a year's work: at one or two a month they fit a twelve-month
calendar and do not fit a six-month one.

**They are the easiest deliverable in this file to do badly**, so the rules
are tighter than for anything else:

- **Pick places the client actually serves, from their own catchment.** The
  rank grid (`local-and-gmb.md`) shows where visibility already falls off;
  those neighbourhoods are the candidates. A page for an area nobody travels
  from is a page nobody reads.
- **Twelve to sixteen, not forty.** The ceiling is how many genuinely
  distinct pages can be written, and that is set by how much real local
  detail exists — not by how many names are on a map. A client whose city
  has eight neighbourhoods worth writing about gets eight.
- **Every page needs the `About {Place}` section filled with verified local
  fact**: the courthouse, division, station or hospital that serves it *by
  name*, the arterial road or transit stop, what the area is actually like.
  `seo-content-production/references/content-templates.md` Template 3 marks
  that section as the one that makes the page not a doorway page. If you
  cannot write it without inventing, the neighbourhood does not get a page.
- **Never a find-and-replace of the city page.** Two pages whose only
  difference is a place name are a doorway-page pattern, Google has said so
  for years, and the client's own developer can diff them.
- **They link up to the service page and sideways to two or three genuinely
  adjacent areas**, which is what makes them a network rather than a pile.

Word count is Template 3's: 1,500 words, floor 1,200. A 2,000-word
neighbourhood page is padding, and padding is exactly what makes this page
type look like what it must not be.

## What never goes in either plan

**A price.** Not in the strategy document, not in the audit, not in the table
above. Plans and their contents live here; what they cost is a conversation
between the client and whoever is selling, and a figure written into a
deliverable is a figure somebody has to honour a year later.

**A guaranteed outcome.** Both plans commit to work, and to targets the
strategy argues for in writing. Neither commits to a ranking, a volume of
leads, or a return. That is a commitment to clients, not a style preference —
`seo-content-production/references/house-standards.md` §12 has the full
claims policy.
