# How a strategy document has to read

A strategy nobody can follow is not a strategy. It is a document.

The failure this file prevents is specific. It is not bad writing in the usual
sense. No typos, no grammar errors, every sentence defensible — and a client who
reads it three times still cannot say what we are going to do.

The test is one question, asked of every section: **after reading this, could
the client say out loud what we are doing, and could someone on our team start
it on Monday?** If the answer is no, the section is not finished, however
accurate it is.

---

## Who this file governs

**Every document a client reads.** The audit, the strategy, the monthly report,
the content brief, the onboarding pages and the portal. One voice across all of
them. The other skills point here rather than keeping their own copy.

---

## Rule 1 — The document is for the client, not for the reviewer

This is the rule that was missing, and it caused more damage than anything
else in this file.

A strategy gets reviewed internally before it ships. The reviewer pushes back,
the next draft answers the pushback, and the answer stays in the document. Do
that a dozen times and the client receives a document arguing with somebody who
is not in the room.

Measured across the two documents this spec was built from: the agency we are
measured against does this **zero** times in 5,893 words. Ours did it **eighteen**
times in 7,420.

**What it looks like.** All of these shipped:

| Shipped | What is wrong | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| One thing we checked that is worth your knowing: neither competitor got there on links. | Announcing that a check happened. The client assumes we checked. | Neither competitor got there on links. |
| The 551 figure needs explaining, because it looks like a strength and is not. | Defending a number against an objection nobody raised. | Of the 551 sites linking to the firm, 547 are automated junk. |
| Read the last two columns together, because they are the finding. | Instructing the reader how to read our table. | Citadel Law has 216 reviews and ranks 25.8. Mira Law has 12 and does not appear. |
| We would rather give you 96 rows you can build than pad four more. | Justifying our own methodology. | 96 searches, each with a page that owns it. |
| That is the honest answer, not a gap in it. | Pre-empting an accusation. | *(delete — say the thing and stop)* |
| This appendix is here so it is clear the question was asked, not overlooked. | Defending the document's completeness. | No previous SEO work has been done on this site. |

**The test.** Read any sentence and ask: *is this for Mira Bhandari, or is it for
the person who might criticise the draft?* If it is the second, cut it. The
client did not attend the review and does not need the transcript.

**The same fault in another form.** A question asked in a working session must
never become a paragraph in the deliverable. Somebody asks "why are there 'near
me' keywords in here?" and the next draft grows a section explaining why "near
me" keywords are in there. That explanation belongs in the reply to the
question, not in the client's document. The client never asked.

### The month table is read first, and read by a non-specialist

Everything else in this file is about sentences. The month table has no
sentences, which is exactly why it goes wrong: twelve rows of three-word
cells, written at the speed of someone who already knows the plan.

A real table shipped with these cells: **"4 Tier 1 listings"**, **"Q&A
seeded"**, **"Profile photo set expanded"**, **"Speed work: the 9.0-second
render path"**, **"Internal link text repaired"**. The client who received it
said he could not understand his own plan, and he runs the agency.

**Every cell says what a person would do, in words the client used.**

| Shipped | Write instead |
|---|---|
| 4 Tier 1 listings | Claim the four professional listings the firm is entitled to |
| Q&A seeded | Write and answer eight questions on the Google profile |
| Profile photo set expanded | Add six more photographs to the profile |
| Speed work: the 9.0-second render path | Cut the time the homepage takes to look finished |
| Internal link text repaired | Rewrite the wording of the links between pages |
| 1 citable resource published | Publish one guide other sites would link to |
| Grid re-measured | Re-run the map ranking check across the GTA |

No tier numbers. No abbreviations the client did not use. Nothing in the
passive. If a cell needs a term of art, the term does not belong in the table.

`scripts/check_strategy_doc.py` carries a list of the shorthand we are known
to reach for and fails on it. That list is a floor, not the rule: it cannot
know the shorthand we invent next week. The rule is the table above.

### Put the client's stated priority in month one

If the client named a goal — first-page results in Brampton — month one shows
work against it. Not the whole build; the part that can start immediately.
Brampton's titles, the Brampton service area on the profile, the keyword set
for it.

A plan that opens with five months of groundwork before touching the thing
the client asked for reads as a plan about us. The sequencing can still be
foundation-first underneath, and usually should be. What changes is that the
client can find their own priority in month one and watch it move.

## Rule 2 — Pronouns: we do the work, the firm owns the business

**The ratios, measured.** The agency we are measured against says "you" or
"your" 5.3 times per 1,000 words and names the client's firm 7.6 times. Ours
said "you" 23.3 times and named the firm 2.4. We jab the reader four times as
often and name them three times less. That is the whole "it is talking at me"
feeling, in two numbers.

| Use | For | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **we, our, us** | Everything we do, and everything we are doing together | "We start with the pages and the profile you already have." "Our ranking goals around the office." |
| **the firm's name** | Facts, measurements, anything checkable | "Mira Law's website got 173 clicks in November." |
| **you, your** | Only things that are genuinely and only theirs | "your name", "your office", "your Google Business Profile" |

**Never:** "your company", "your business", "your firm" as a way of saying "you".
Use the name. It is shorter, warmer and it is what a person would say.

**Targets:** "you / your" under 14 per 1,000 words. The firm's name at least
as often as "you".

**The limit used to be 8, and that was too tight.** The benchmark runs 3.0
and uses "you" warmly — *"enough information for you to understand"*,
*"you'll receive monthly performance reports"*, *"based on our research and
your feedback"*. Those are the warmest sentences in the document. A limit
tight enough to make a writer avoid them is working against the voice we
want.

The word was never the problem. Institutional coldness was, and what fixes
that is contractions, one idea per paragraph, and a sentence that puts
somebody in the work. Those are the rules that replaced it.

**Still never:** "your company", "your business", "your firm" as a way of
saying "you". Use the name.

**Why "we" reads as warmer.** "We" says two parties are doing this. "You"
with no "we" beside it says one party is being assessed by another. A client
paying for twelve months of work should read the first.

## Rule 3 — A strategy is not an audit

**The audit says what is true now. The strategy says what we are going to do.**
A client can forward the strategy to their partners; the audit is the document
that explains why they hired us, and they only need to read it once.

**Banned from a strategy document:** any present-tense statement of a
deficiency.

| Never in a strategy | Why | Say instead |
|---|---|---|
| No page title contains the word "lawyer". | A finding. The audit already said it. | We rewrite the titles on all 23 pages in month one. |
| The services list is empty. | A finding. | We add nine services to the profile in month one. |
| 426 words today. | A finding. | We take the sexual assault page to 2,000 words in month two. |
| Your citation score is 6 out of 100. | A third-party grade on the current state. | We get 20 listings live and correct by month three. |
| The thirteen city pages are 53–61% the same text. | A finding. | We rewrite four city pages around their own courthouses. |

**A number is allowed when it is half of a projection.** That is the whole
test. A baseline exists to show the size of the jump, so it appears beside the
target it is being measured against and nowhere else.

> **Allowed.** We start from 173 clicks a month and aim for 400 by month six.

> **Allowed.**
>
> | What we measure | Today | Month 6 | Month 12 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Clicks from searches that are not the firm's name | 1 | 40 | 120 |

> **Never.** Of the 139 attributable clicks, 138 were somebody typing the firm's
> name. One was not.

The second version states a baseline with no target attached, so it is a
finding, and findings belong in the audit.

**What the strategy opens with.** Not what is wrong. What we are going to do,
in what order, and what it is expected to produce. `document-assembly.md` has
the running order.

### No findings section, whatever it is called

The rule above is about sentences. This one is about chapters, and it is the
half that actually went wrong.

A strategy came back with a section headed **"Where the firm stands today"** —
323 words of measured present, every figure sourced and dated, not one target
anywhere. Every sentence in it was true. The checker passed it, because the
checker was reading sentences.

**A heading that announces the present is a findings chapter.** "Where the firm
stands today", "Current performance", "Our findings", "Today's numbers". The
plan's version of that content is a table with a Target column. If a section
carries measured figures and commits to no month and no target, it belongs in
the audit.

**"Finding" is the audit's word for the audit's content.** A plan has
priorities, targets and months. `scripts/check_strategy_doc.py` fails on it.

**What is still allowed in a section about the present**, because this is a
real distinction and the checker was taught it:

- *Market demand* — "an estimated 2,900 people a month search dui lawyer" — is
  the raw material of a plan, not a measurement of the client. A keyword
  opportunity table is the most forward-looking thing in the document.
- *A baseline beside its target*, in any shape: a Today / Month 6 / Month 12
  table, or "173 clicks a month today, 400 by month six".

## Read this part first: a strategy is a letter, not a spec sheet

This file was calibrated against the wrong document for a year, and four of
its targets were the wrong sign as a result. Worth stating plainly, because
the error is easy to repeat.

The numbers in the old version of this section were measured against a
competitor's **audit** — a technical reference document written for an agency
audience. An audit is looked things up in, so it is dense, heavily labelled
and written in the imperative. All of that is correct for an audit.

A strategy document is read front to back by somebody who is not a
specialist, and then forwarded to a partner who was in none of the meetings.
The two genres want opposite things. We specified the first and shipped four
drafts of it while the client asked for the second.

**`assets/client-letter-benchmark.md` is the voice.** Read it before writing
anything. What it measures, against what we were producing:

| Measured over prose | Benchmark | Ours, rejected | Target now |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sentences per paragraph** | **1.1** | 2.8 | **under 1.9** |
| **Contractions per 1,000 words** | **24.5** | 6.1 | **10+** |
| `we'll` against `We will` | 10 / 0 | 0 / 12 | prefer `we'll` |
| Paragraphs opening with a fragment | 3% | 44% | **under 10%** |
| Bare imperatives per 1,000 words | 0.0 | 6.1 | **under 2.0** |
| Unhedged four-figure estimates | 0 | 4 | **0** |
| Cleft sentences | 0.00 | 1.03 per 1k | **0** |
| "rather than" / "not X but Y" | 0.00 | 4.27 per 1k | under 1 per 1k |
| Sentences over 25 words | 24% | 3% | **not counted** |
| Longest sentence | 42 words | 30 | under 40 |
| Flesch-Kincaid grade | 14.5 | 7.0 | **not a target** |

Read the last two rows together before trusting any readability number
again. The document the client calls readable is **harder** by every standard
metric and uses **longer** sentences than the one he gave up on. Grade level,
sentence length and word length cannot see whatever makes writing readable,
and four of them were gates in this skill until this rewrite.

What survived the recalibration: the banned constructions below. Those are
about **shape**, and the benchmark scores zero on all of them. Shape was
always the real fault. Length, labels, imperatives and reading grade were
not, and chasing them is what produced a telegram.

## The five constructions that are banned

Each one is grammatical. Each one makes the reader hold the first half of the
sentence in their head until the second half arrives. Each one is in the
November document and in none of the competitor's 117 pages.

### 1. The cleft — the subject arrives late, or not at all

> **Don't:** The useful part is not that they are ahead. It is how.
> **Do:** They are ahead because they built a page for each offence in each city. We have not built those pages.

> **Don't:** A hub that links to its children using each child's own search term is what tells Google which page owns which search.
> **Do:** Link each hub to its children using the child's own search term. That tells Google which page owns which search.

### 2. The fronted subordinate clause

> **Don't:** Where a larger search sits in work you did not name, it waits behind a smaller search in work you did.
> **Do:** You named sexual assault and impaired driving as your most profitable work. We build those pages first, even where another area has more searches.

Start the sentence with its subject. If a condition has to come first, make it
its own sentence.

### 3. "Rather than" and "not X but Y"

These were the single most common marker in the November document — 29 of them
in 6,794 words, against zero in the competitor's. They turn one fact into a
comparison the reader has to resolve.

> **Don't:** Each one is a stream of work rather than a single job.
> **Do:** Each one runs for several months. It is not a single job.

> **Don't:** The sequence is commercial rather than geographic.
> **Do:** We ordered the cities by what their searches are worth.

### 4. "is what", "is why", "is the one"

> **Don't:** Naming the actual courthouse is what makes a location page a real page.
> **Do:** Name the actual courthouse on every location page. Without it the page is a template with the city swapped.

### 5. The verb that never arrives

> **Don't:** Alongside these run the work that has no single milestone.
> **Do:** Four things run all year: 100 articles, a weekly Google Business post, monthly reporting, and the quarterly review.

## The shape of a paragraph

**One idea, one paragraph. Usually one sentence.**

This is the single most important rule in the file, and it replaces a rule
that said the opposite. The benchmark runs **1.1 sentences per paragraph** —
almost every sentence gets its own line and white space around it. Ours ran
2.8, because this file used to say "two to four sentences, then stop".

That density is what made the labels necessary. A reader facing a wall of
three-sentence blocks needs a handle on each one, so labels got bolted on,
and then a rule demanded more of them. Fix the paragraph length and the
labels stop being needed at all.

In a 60-page document this is what decides whether anybody reaches the end.

### No fragment openers

Our signature habit, and the one the client gave up on. 44% of the paragraphs
in the rejected strategy opened with a verbless fragment and a full stop:

> **Never:** The year's totals. The plan delivers 4 service pages, 24 city
> location pages and 1 resource guide.

> **Write:** The plan delivers 4 service pages, 24 city location pages and 1
> resource guide.

The fragment is a heading nobody asked for. `The year's totals.`, `How each
keyword is scored.`, `Who is reading.`, `Claim the memberships first.`, `The
wider GTA.` — all shipped, all cut.

The old label check could not see any of it, because it only matched a colon
or a bold run. It scored our 44% document at 0% and told the model to add
more labels. `scripts/check_strategy_doc.py` now fails above 10%.

### Labels: a ceiling, not a floor

A label earns its place in a **specification block** — a meta standards row,
a schema table, a linking rule somebody looks up. It does not belong on
narrative prose, and this file used to demand it on a quarter of every
document. The ceiling is now 15% and there is no floor.

> **Correct, in a specification block.** **Title tags:** `[Primary Keyword]
> [Location] | Mira Law`. Under 60 characters. Unique on every page.

### The rules for the sentence itself

- **Contract what a person would contract.** `we'll`, `we've`, `I've`,
  `isn't`, `you'll`. The benchmark runs 24.5 contractions per 1,000 words;
  ours ran 6.1, with twelve `We will` and not one `we'll`. A document with no
  contractions reads as a notice, and this is a large share of what the
  client meant by "humans don't talk like this". It is not informality — the
  benchmark is a professional document and it contracts constantly.
- **`We'll` and the verb. Never the bare verb.** `We'll profile the render
  path in month 4.` Not `Profile the render path in month 4`, which is a line
  from our worklist. The benchmark uses **zero** bare imperatives in prose;
  ours used 6.1 per 1,000 words, because this file demanded 4+. Imperatives
  stay correct inside a specification row.
- **Future work takes `will` or `we'll`.** Not `We optimize the website in
  month one`, which is the present tense for something that has not happened,
  and a reader stops on it.
- **Complete sentences.** Subject, main verb. `A separate set of 40, in
  keywords-gmb.csv.` is a stub; `A separate set of 40 keywords sits in
  keywords-gmb.csv.` is a sentence.
- **Say the noun.** `A separate set of 40` leaves the reader working out 40
  of what. `40 keywords` costs one word and saves a re-read.
- **Say what we will do with it.** A paragraph that only describes a
  deliverable is half-written. `The profile answers them, not a page`
  describes. `We'll optimize the profile for these searches, because
  "criminal lawyer near me" alone gets about 1,000 a month` commits.
- **Lead with the reader's situation.** `To put things into perspective …`,
  `For example, someone searching for "DUI Lawyer Brampton" …`, `Think of
  this as making sure Google properly understands …`. Every technical point
  arrives attached to a person doing something.

## Sentence discipline

- **Length is not a rule.** This file used to say "12–18 words on average,
  nothing over 25", and the checker failed any document with more than 10% of
  its sentences over 25 words. The benchmark runs **24% over 25 words**,
  averages 19.2 and peaks at 42. Shape is what costs a re-read; length is
  not. 40 words is the only ceiling now, and it is a review rather than a
  failure.
- **A long sentence has to run in one direction.** The benchmark's 42-word
  sentence is a list: subject, verb, three parallel things. That reads
  straight through. A 20-word sentence that doubles back on itself does not.
- **Subject, verb, object.** In that order, in almost every sentence. This is
  the rule the banned constructions below all protect.
- **One em-dash per section, at most.** The rejected document used 49 in
  6,794 words. Each one is a clause the reader has to hold open.
- **No reading-grade target.** Flesch-Kincaid is reported and ignored. The
  benchmark scores 14.5; the document the client gave up on scored 7.0.
- **Tables for anything with more than three parallel items.** A paragraph
  listing eight keywords is unreadable; the same eight in a table are
  scannable and can go straight to a developer.

## Round every estimate, and keep the word "estimated"

Two separate rules that pull in the same direction.

**Round it.** The benchmark writes `approximately 4,500 organic visits per
month` and `approximately $23,600/month`. Ours wrote `4,501 visits` and
`$23,655`. Both are Ahrefs estimates of a competitor's traffic, so the last
three digits are noise, and printing them is most of what makes our prose
read like a lab report.

It is also an accuracy problem. A client who checks `4,501` against a real
figure of 4,120 has caught us being precisely wrong instead of roughly right.

**Keep the hedge.** `estimated`, `approximately`, `about`. This is a
commitment in `CLAUDE.md`, not a style choice: never state a client's own
traffic as fact from Ahrefs, because it is the one number they can check
against an analytics tab they already have open.

The benchmark does both at once — `receives an estimated 4,500 organic visits
per month` — which is the proof that honesty and confidence are not in
tension. Confidence comes from knowing what we'll do about the number, not
from pretending to four significant figures.

**Tables keep their digits.** A keyword volume column, an Opportunity Score,
a page count. Those are specifications, and `check_strategy_doc.py` measures
prose only.

## No provenance, no appendices

The client asked for this out, in these words: *"We don't need to tell the
client where everything is coming from. The information we are giving to
client should be fundamentally confidence giving. We don't need appendixes.
We have already onboarded the client and this is a strategy document."*

Out, therefore:

| Shipped | Why it goes |
|---|---|
| `All the figures below are Ahrefs estimates taken on 21 November 2026.` | An audit trail for a reviewer. Rule 1 in another costume |
| `measured on 21 November 2026` | A pull date. The client does not audit our sources |
| `Appendix A — Where every figure came from` | Same, with a heading |
| A `Measured or estimated` column | Say `estimated` in the sentence instead |
| `(Ahrefs, 9 Nov 2026)` after a baseline | Drop the citation, keep the word |

The sources, dates and methods stay in our own notes and in the portal, where
they are genuinely needed. They are how we answer a challenge; they are not
the document.

## The three questions every section answers, in this order

1. **What is true right now.** One rounded number, in a sentence a
   non-specialist follows. Not its period and not its source — see *No
   provenance* above.
2. **What we are going to do about it.** A named, countable action, with
   `we'll` in front of it. Not the bare imperative: `We'll write 24 location
   pages`, never `Write 24 location pages`.
3. **What changes if we do it.** The mechanism — *why* the action moves the
   number. Not a promised figure.

A section missing (1) is an opinion. Missing (2) is an observation. Missing (3)
is a task list the client cannot evaluate or argue with.

## The six ways our prose goes wrong

These are older than the shape rules above and still apply. Each is invisible to
a spell-check.

### 1. Abstraction stacking

> **Don't:** Implementation of a topical authority framework will establish
> semantic relevance signals across the practice-area taxonomy.

> **Do:** You have one page about bail hearings. Your three main competitors
> have between four and nine each. We write six more, one per stage of a bail
> hearing.

### 2. Nouns doing a verb's job

| Don't | Do |
|---|---|
| Implementation of schema markup | Add LocalBusiness schema to all 14 pages |
| Optimization of the GMB listing | Rewrite the 750-character description. Add all 9 services |
| Content development across priority clusters | Publish 12 pages, 3 a month, starting with bail hearings |
| Utilization of internal linking opportunities | Link every new page to the service page above it and two siblings |

If a sentence's main verb is "is", "will be", or "involves", the real verb is
trapped in a noun. Let it out.

### 3. No subject — nobody is doing it

> **Don't:** Pages will be optimized and schema will be deployed in month two.
> **Do:** We optimize the pages in month two. You need to give us WordPress
> editor access first. That is the one thing we need from you.

**Say "we" for what 6ix Developers does and "you" for what the client does.**

### 4. Jargon with no gloss

Some terms have to appear. The rule is not to avoid them. **Each one is defined
once, in plain words, in the sentence where it first appears.**

> **Domain Rating** — Ahrefs' 0–100 estimate of how much other sites link to
> you — is 4. The three firms you named are at 22, 31 and 38.

A glossary at the back does not count. Nobody flips to the back.

### 5. A number with no period, no unit, and no source

Every figure carries three things or it does not go in: what it measures, over
what period, and where it came from. `seo-audit-report/references/measured-vs-estimated.md`
is the long version. A figure from the client's own GA4 or Search Console is a
measurement. An Ahrefs figure about the client's own site is an estimate and
must say so.

### 6. Recommendations that are not instructions

> **Don't:** Consider improving page speed where possible.
> **Do:** Your homepage takes 4.8 seconds to become usable on mobile, measured
> by PageSpeed Insights on 4 November. Three uncompressed hero images cause most
> of it. We compress them and move to WebP in week one. Target: under 2.5
> seconds.

## What confidence sounds like

The client is paying for a decision, not a survey of possibilities.

| Overclaiming — never | Hedging — reads as weak | Confident and true |
|---|---|---|
| This will generate 40 new leads a month | This may help improve visibility over time | These 6 pages target 2,900 monthly searches that send you nothing today |
| You will rank #1 within 90 days | Rankings could potentially improve | 31 of your target keywords sit on page 2. Those move first |
| Guaranteed 3× return | Results vary by market | The three firms ahead of you have 4–9 pages per practice area. You have 1 |

**State the gap, in numbers, and name what closes it.**

## Section titles: plain and orienting

This section used to recommend the opposite, and it was wrong twice over.

It asked for titles like *"Your site blocks Google from a third of its own
pages"* and *"You appear in 4 of 25 map searches across the GTA"*. Those are
**audit headlines** — a present-tense deficiency, which Rule 3 bans in the
body and should equally ban in a heading. They also jab the reader with
"you" in the one place they cannot skip.

The second fault is navigation. In a 60-page document a reader uses the
contents page to find things, and a clever title is unfindable. Ours shipped
*"Who is ahead, and how"*, *"Keywords"*, *"The page plan"*, *"Appearing in AI
answers"*. The benchmark uses boring titles that say what the chapter is:

| Ours, rejected | Benchmark |
|---|---|
| Who is ahead, and how | Current SEO Performance & Opportunities |
| Keywords | Keyword Research & Targeting Strategy |
| Changes to the pages you already have | Website Optimization & Technical SEO |
| The page plan | Service Pages & Location-Based SEO |
| Links and authority | Content Development & SEO Authority |
| Objectives, and how we will know | Our 12-Month SEO Objectives |
| Reporting | Ongoing SEO Management & Reporting |

Dull, parallel, and a partner can find the one chapter they care about in
four seconds. That is the whole job of a title in a long document.

Keep the interesting claim for the first sentence underneath it, where it has
room to be true.

## Before it goes out

Read a page out loud. Not metaphorically. Every sentence you stumble on is a
sentence the client will read twice and then skip.

Then the mechanical pass:

```bash
python scripts/check_strategy_doc.py <document.md>
```

It measures sentence shape, paragraph length, fragment openers, contraction
rate, bare imperatives, unhedged estimates and provenance clutter against the
table at the top of this file. It cannot tell you the document is clear. It
can tell you where it is definitely not.

**Calibration rule, and it is not optional.** Every check in that script is
run against three documents before it ships: the rejected strategy (must
fail), the rewrite (must pass), and `assets/client-letter-benchmark.md` (must
pass). A check that fails the benchmark is wrong, whatever it is measuring —
four of them did, for a year, and that is why the output was unreadable. Fix
the check, then add the case to `tools/tests/test_strategy_language.py`.

The thing it cannot check, and the one that matters most: **hand the document to
somebody who was not in the research and ask them what we are going to do.** If
they cannot answer, no amount of accuracy in it has done its job.
